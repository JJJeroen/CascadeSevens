# Cascade Break — Design Document

Status: **Design in progress, web rules mock playable, no production app code yet.** Working title / codename during dev was *Zevenen*. Original rules were dictated in Dutch and are translated + disambiguated below; several points weren't fully specified in the source rules and are decided or flagged open per §3/§9. This document is the spec the rules engine is built against.

**Revision note (2026-07-23):** an adversarial design review (brutalcritic) found a genuine self-contradiction in the original draft of the opening-turn exception (§2.5) and two unaddressed rule-interaction exploits between the come-out gate (§2.4) and the shared tableau (§3) — a player could otherwise rack up meld points or even satisfy come-out without ever meeting the 40-point bar, hollowing out the mechanic that's the game's whole identity (§4). All three are now resolved below; see §3 for the added decisions. The naming call (§6) was also downgraded from "chosen" to "working title" pending fuller diligence.

**Revision note (2026-07-26):** a playtest of the web rules mock (§7 roadmap phase 0, built and deployed) surfaced several places where the 2026-07-23 draft didn't match the actual physical game, confirmed against the game's designer, across three rounds of feedback the same day. First round: the open-row pickup is available every turn regardless of come-out status, not gated behind it (§2.3, §2.5 — this also retires §3 decision 5, which turns out to have been fixing a deadlock that a voluntary (not forced) pickup never actually had); the Turn 0 exchange is asymmetric, not symmetric (§2.6); melding away your entire hand in Part 2 is illegal outright rather than an instant win (§2.7, §3); the tableau's shared-access rule was correct, but "shared" undersold it — either player can also pull cards back out of any meld and re-lay them into different valid melds, a real Rummy-style manipulation mechanic, not just additions (§2.3, §3). Second round, from actually playing the corrected mock: the open-row take may be repeated within Part 1 itself, locking out the closed pile once used, with only the most recent take's obligation binding (§2.3, §3 decision 9). Third round, prompted by the mock's live-score display surfacing a below-threshold come-out attempt mid-game: come-out progress toward 40 carries forward across turns rather than resetting each turn (§2.4, §3 decision 10). See the added §3 decisions and §9 for the full list.

**Revision note (2026-07-27):** a fourth round of live-play feedback caught a real rule gap the previous day's rearrangement design missed: pulling from the tableau is restricted to cards the pulling player currently owns, not any card regardless of who laid it (§2.3, §3 decision 11). This also **retracts a claim from the 2026-07-26 revision** — §2.8 and the §4 "tableau rearrangement is a second score-swing mechanic" note both previously said rearrangement could move scoring credit between players the same way a joker swap can; that's wrong under the ownership restriction and has been corrected in place (rearranging only ever touches cards already credited to the rearranging player, so it can't move credit at all — joker swap-out remains the only mechanic that does).

A later same-day round added one more: adding a card to a run may reposition an existing joker in that run rather than only ever extending an end (§2.3, §3 decision 12) — e.g. a run of JOKER(as 9),10,J can become 10,J,QUEEN(joker),K when a K arrives, sliding what the joker stands for instead of rejecting the K.

**Revision note (2026-07-27, later the same day):** a full tableau rearrange session was added alongside the existing single-card pull (§2.3, §3 decision 13) — a draft-then-commit mode where any card on the table (either player's) plus the player's own hand can be freely regrouped, including through temporarily-invalid states, with validation deferred until commit. Two invariants (pre-existing cards always keep their original owner; a non-owned pre-existing card can't be left in hand) keep this more-permissive session from reopening the exploit the single-pull's ownership restriction was built to close.

**Revision note (2026-07-27, a fifth round):** two more mock-vs-physical-game mismatches, both about who acts when. First: which player starts had defaulted to a fixed player (P1) for every game and every round — confirmed against the designer, the real game decides round 1's starter **by chance**, and it alternates every round after that (§2.2, §3 decision 14). Second: §2.6's Turn 0 exchange was written assuming Turn 1 always follows the fixed P1→P2→P1… rotation regardless of what happened in Turn 0 — but taking the starter card and swapping "uses up" that player's go the same as a normal turn does, so whoever made the **last accepted swap** in Turn 0 hands Turn 1 to their opponent, not to themselves; only if nobody ever accepted anything does the original starter begin Turn 1 as before (§2.6, §3 decision 15). §2.6 is also reworded throughout from "P1"/"P2" to "the starting player"/"the other player," since the starter is no longer always P1.

**Revision note (2026-07-27, a sixth round — adversarial review):** an independent brutalcritic review (not live playtesting) found a real interaction gap between two already-decided rules that had never been checked against each other: melding your entire hand is illegal outright (§3 decision 8), but a "must meld this card" obligation — from a row-take (§2.5) or a joker swap-out (§2.3) — demands exactly that. If a player's hand ever shrinks to just the still-owed card(s), those two rules directly conflict and the obligation becomes permanently unmeldable, with no discard possible either (discard requires no outstanding obligations). The mock never actually reached this in practice because the AI's own heuristics happened to avoid it, but nothing in the *rules* (or the engine) prevented a human from reaching it. Resolved by formalizing the missing constraint explicitly: a player may never take an action that would leave their hand at or below the number of cards still owed to an obligation (§3 decision 16). This isn't a change to any existing rule's outcome — it just makes explicit an invariant the game was always implicitly relying on but never stated or enforced.

## 1. Concept

A 2-player card game in the Rummy/Gin-Rummy family, played with a standard 52-card deck plus 2 jokers (54 cards). Its distinguishing mechanic — not present in standard Rummy or Gin Rummy — is the **open row**: a single, growing, face-up sequence of discarded cards, each overlapping the last (like a solitaire cascade). A player may draw from the closed deck, *or* reach into the open row and take any card in it — but doing so forces them to also take every card discarded after it (everything visually stacked on top). That taken card must then be used in a meld that same turn. This turns discarding into a genuine tactical decision: bury a card the opponent wants deep enough, and taking it costs them a pile.

**Why this app:** the physical game (a Dutch family variant, working name "Zevenen"/"Duizend leggen"-adjacent) has no digital implementation. Building it digitally also removes the game's biggest real-world friction — manually counting hand/meld points every round — which a screen does instantly and without dispute.

Solo (vs. AI) first, matching the Appiness playbook: GitHub-hosted APK for sideload testing, then Expo EAS build to Google Play once stable. Multiplayer is a deliberate later phase — see §5.

## 2. Rules

### 2.1 Deck & card values

- One standard 52-card deck + 2 jokers = 54 cards.
- Values (used for meld scoring and hand-penalty scoring, *not* for meld-forming rank):
  - 2–9: **5 points**
  - 10–K: **10 points**
  - Ace: **25 points**
  - Joker: **50 points**

### 2.2 Setup (per round)

1. Shuffle all 54 cards.
2. Deal 7 cards to each player.
3. Flip the top card of the remaining deck face-up — this is the first card of the **open row**. The rest is the **closed pile** (draw pile).
4. **Who starts:** round 1 of a game is decided by chance (a coin flip); every round after that, the starter alternates from the previous round's starter, regardless of who won that round (§3 decision 14, added 2026-07-27). "Starting" a round means being the player Turn 0 (§2.6) is first offered to, and — if Turn 0 never triggers or resolves with no accepted swap — being the player whose Turn 1 comes first.

### 2.3 Turn structure

Each turn has three parts, always in this order:

**Part 1 — Draw (mandatory, pick one path):**
- Draw the top card of the closed pile into hand — a single card, and this immediately ends Part 1. **Only available if the player hasn't already taken from the open row this turn** (see below). **Or:**
- Take a card from the open row into hand — **available every turn, regardless of come-out status** (§3, revised 2026-07-26). Taking a card from the open row takes **every card discarded after it** (i.e. everything lying partially on top of it) — see §2.5 for the exact mechanic and the Turn 0 starter-card exchange. This is a voluntary choice: a player who can't legally meld the card they'd be obligated to play (§2.5) simply shouldn't take it and should draw from the closed pile instead — there's no forced pickup, so this never produces an unplayable position.
  - **The open row may be taken from repeatedly within the same Part 1** (added 2026-07-26) — once a player takes from the row at all, the closed pile is locked out for the rest of that turn, but the player may keep taking from the row as many times as they choose. Only the **most recent** take's bottom card is the binding "must meld" obligation (§2.5) — an earlier take's obligation is superseded, not accumulated, the moment another row-take happens (the earlier scooped cards are still in hand, just no longer individually obligated). The player explicitly ends Part 1 and moves to Part 2 once they're done drawing.

**Part 2 — Meld actions (optional, any order, any number of times) — all except laying a new meld require the player to have already come out (§2.4); a player who hasn't come out yet can only lay new melds from hand, nothing else:**
- Lay down a new meld from hand. A meld is either:
  - **A set**: 3+ cards of the same rank, or
  - **A run**: 3+ cards in sequence, same suit. Ace can anchor either end of a run (A-2-3 or Q-K-A), but never both at once — no wraparound (K-A-2 is not a legal run). (§9, resolved)
  - **A meld action can never leave the player with 0 cards in hand** — this is illegal outright, not merely undesirable (§2.7, §3, revised 2026-07-26). A player must always keep at least one card to discard in Part 3.
- Add one or more cards from hand to an existing meld on the table — **any player's meld, laid by either player.** Melds are grouped by owner on the table for display (each player's melds sit in their own area), but that's a layout convention, not an access restriction — either player can add to either area (§3, revised 2026-07-26). Adding to a meld, own or opponent's, requires the adding player to have already come out; it cannot itself substitute for coming out (see §3, decided). **For a run specifically, if the new card doesn't directly extend either end but a joker already in that run could be repositioned to make room, that's allowed as a single action** (§3 decision 12, added 2026-07-27) — e.g. a run of JOKER(as 9),10,J can become 10,J,QUEEN(joker),K when a K is added, by reassigning what the joker stands for rather than rejecting the K outright. Ownership of the pre-existing cards (real or joker) is unaffected by the reposition — only the newly added card is credited to the player who added it (§2.8).
- **Rearrange the tableau** (§3, added 2026-07-26; ownership restriction added 2026-07-27): pull one or more cards back out of a meld already on the table and re-lay them into different, still-valid meld(s), exactly like standard Rummy manipulation. **A player may only pull cards they themselves currently own** (per the ownership tracking in §2.8) — pulling a card credited to the opponent is illegal, even though either player can still freely *add* a new card to any meld regardless of who laid it. Requires having already come out. Any meld a card is pulled from must, after the pull, either be fully emptied (dissolved) or still satisfy the normal 3+-card set/run rules — you can never leave a meld sitting at 1 or 2 cards (including the cards *left behind*, which may belong to the other player and are just as bound by this), and pulling a card out of the *middle* of a run (splitting it into two disconnected fragments) is illegal for the same reason. Hand cards can be mixed into the same re-lay. Because pulling is ownership-restricted, rearranging never moves scoring credit between players by itself — a re-laid card was already yours and stays yours (§2.8). Joker swap-out (below) remains the one mechanic where credit can shift from one player to the other, since it has no ownership restriction.
- **Or, start a full rearrange session** (§3 decision 13, added 2026-07-27) — a more powerful alternative to the single-card pull above, for reorganizing across multiple melds at once rather than one card at a time. Within the session, any card on the table (either player's) plus the player's own hand can be freely regrouped, including into temporarily-invalid groupings — nothing is checked until the player commits, at which point every group is validated; if something's wrong, the player is told specifically what and can keep adjusting or cancel back to exactly the pre-session state. A pre-existing card always keeps its original owner regardless of which group it ends up in (only new-from-hand cards are credited to the committing player), and a pre-existing card that isn't the committing player's own can't just be left in hand at commit.
- Replace a joker sitting in a meld on the table with the real card it stands in for, taking the joker into hand. The replaced-out joker **must itself be played back onto the table in a meld that same turn** — it cannot just sit in hand afterward. No limit on how often the same joker can be swapped and replayed across a round — this is a deliberate skill/tempo battle, not a bug (see §3, decided).
- **Jokers are wild from the start**: a joker can be placed directly into a new or existing meld as a stand-in for any missing rank/suit card, not only via the swap-out action above (see §3, decided).

**Part 3 — Discard (mandatory):**
- Play one card from hand face-up, placed partially overlapping the current last card of the open row, extending the row. All cards in the row must remain at least partially visible at all times.
- Because Part 2 can never empty a player's hand (see above), there is no longer a mid-turn round-ending exception here — a round only ever ends via this Part 3 discard. (§2.7, revised 2026-07-26)

### 2.4 "Coming out" (first meld of the round)

A player comes out by laying one or more brand-new melds **built entirely from cards in their own hand** (not by adding to any existing meld — see below) whose combined value crosses one of:
- Total value ≥ **40 points**, or
- Be **four cards of the same rank** (a full set of four, regardless of point value — e.g. four 2s = only 20 points but still satisfies this route).

**This total carries forward across turns, confirmed against the designer (2026-07-26) — it is not a single-turn-only requirement.** If a player's first meld attempt falls short (e.g. a 30-point triple), that 30 isn't wasted: it's still there the next time they lay a new meld, and they only need enough more to cross 40 (or hit a four-of-a-kind in one meld) — even if that happens on a later turn. (An earlier draft of this doc assumed the total reset every turn; that was never confirmed and turned out to be wrong — see the 2026-07-26 revision note.)

Until a player has met this bar, they **can still lay additional new melds from hand** — that's how the cumulative total climbs toward 40 — but they **cannot do anything else in Part 2**: no adding cards to any existing meld (own or opponent's), no joker swap, no tableau rearrangement (§2.3). This closes an exploit an adversarial review found in the original draft: without this restriction, a player could add single cards to the *opponent's* already-open melds every turn, scoring full point value per card, indefinitely, without ever meeting the 40-point bar themselves — or even satisfy their own come-out cheaply by dropping one card onto an opponent's existing triple. Neither is allowed (see §3, decided).

### 2.5 The open row & the pickup rule

The open row is an **ordered sequence** of face-up cards, each overlapping the previous one, growing by one card every turn (Part 3). Because each new card is laid on top of the previous one:

- A player may pick **any card in the row**, regardless of come-out status (§2.3, revised 2026-07-26), but must take that card **and every card discarded after it** — i.e. a contiguous run from the chosen card to the current end of the row. You cannot cherry-pick a single buried card without also taking everything stacked above it.
- Whatever card the player chose as the *bottom* of that scooped-up group (the oldest card in the group — the one they actually reached for) **must be used in Part 2 of that same turn**, in a meld. The other, newer cards taken along with it can be used or simply added to hand with no obligation.

### 2.6 Turn 0 — the starter-card exchange

**Rewritten during the 2026-07-23 review** to fix a self-contradiction in the original draft (the original text promised the second player could take "that same single card" after a full turn had already buried it under a mandatory discard, which is impossible under the cascade rule above). This is now modeled as a standalone exchange that happens **once, before normal turn rotation begins** — it is not part of anyone's Part 1/2/3 turn. **Corrected again 2026-07-26: the exchange is asymmetric, not symmetric** — a follow-up "second look" only exists for the starting player, and only along the path where the starting player declined first. Terms below are relative to whoever starts the round (§2.2 decision 14, added 2026-07-27) — "the starting player" and "the other player" — since the starter is no longer always the same seat:

1. The starting player may take the lone starter card into hand, exempt from the "must use it in a meld" obligation (§2.5) and from the come-out gate (§2.4) — this exchange never touches Part 2 at all. **If the starting player takes it, Turn 0 ends immediately after they place one card from hand back onto the row** (a straight one-for-one swap, no meld phase) — there is no follow-up offer to the other player in this path. If the starting player declines, go to step 2.
2. The other player may take that same lone starter card under the identical exemption, then immediately places one card from hand back onto the row. If they decline, go to step 4 (Turn 0 doesn't trigger). If they take it, go to step 3.
3. Because the starting player passed on the card the other player just took, the starting player gets one consolation look: they may take the newly-placed single card, once, under the same exemption, and must also immediately place one card from hand back onto the row. Turn 0 ends after this regardless of whether they accept or decline.
4. If both players decline in steps 1–2, Turn 0 does not trigger at all — the original starter card simply stands as the row, and normal Turn 1 begins with the starting player, with all rules (including the come-out gate and pickup obligation) applying in full.

Because every acceptance in this exchange is a strict one-for-one swap, the row always contains exactly one card at the end of Turn 0, regardless of path — this is what makes the sequence internally consistent (the original draft broke this invariant).

**Who plays Turn 1 (revised 2026-07-27, §3 decision 15):** taking the starter card and swapping "uses up" that player's go the same way an ordinary turn does. So Turn 1 goes to the opponent of whoever made the **last accepted swap** in Turn 0 — not automatically back to the starting player:
- Nobody ever accepted (both declined, step 4): the starting player begins Turn 1, as before.
- The starting player accepted immediately in step 1: the other player begins Turn 1.
- The other player accepted in step 2, and the starting player then declined the step-3 consolation look: the starting player begins Turn 1 (the other player's swap in step 2 was the last one, so it's now the starting player's turn).
- The other player accepted in step 2, and the starting player *also* accepted the step-3 consolation look: the other player begins Turn 1 (the starting player's swap in step 3 was the last one).

### 2.7 End of round

**Revised 2026-07-26 — the mid-turn Part 2 ending was wrong.** A round ends the instant a player reaches **0 cards in hand** at the end of Part 3, playing their last card as that turn's mandatory discard. There is no other way for a round to end: because Part 2 meld actions can never leave a player at 0 cards (§2.3, §3 — such a move is illegal outright, not merely undesirable), a player can never run out of cards before reaching Part 3. That player is the round's winner.

### 2.8 Scoring (per round)

- **Winner bonus**: +50 points to the round winner.
- **Meld points**: every card in every meld on the table scores for whichever player actually placed *that specific card* there — this requires per-card ownership tracking, not per-meld, since either player can add to (or, via tableau rearrangement, relocate cards into) any meld (§3). Ownership follows the act of placement: if a card gets moved during a rearrangement, it now scores for whoever re-placed it, not whoever placed it originally (§2.3). A player's meld score for the round = sum of the point values (§2.1) of every card they currently have sitting in a meld under their placement, at round end.
- **Hand penalty**: at round end, the *loser's* remaining hand cards are each worth their point value (§2.1) as **negative** points. The winner has 0 cards, so no penalty applies to them.
- Round score = winner bonus (if applicable) + own meld points − own hand penalty.
- **App behavior (added 2026-07-26)**: the app displays a live running score — an intermediate per-turn tally of meld points earned as they happen during Part 2, plus each player's persistent total (carried over from previous rounds) — rather than only revealing the round's score in one lump sum at round end. This is a display/UX choice, not a change to *when* the round-ending winner-bonus/hand-penalty math above is actually computed (still only resolvable once the round ends).

### 2.9 End of game

The game ends at the end of whichever round is the first in which one player has **more than 1000 total points** *and* has strictly more points than the other player. That player wins the game.

**No separate tie-break rule is needed (§9, resolved)**: this two-part condition is simply re-checked after every round. If it isn't met by exactly one player — both are over 1000 but tied, or one player is ahead but neither has crossed 1000, or the trailing player crossed 1000 without also being ahead — the game just continues to another round. No sudden-death or special-case logic required; the existing condition is self-resolving by construction.

**Game modes (§9, resolved)**: v1 offers two score targets, selectable before starting a game:
- **Standard**: first to (>1000 and strictly ahead), as above.
- **Quick**: same win condition, lower threshold (e.g. >300 and strictly ahead) — for a shorter mobile session. Purely a parameterized threshold, no other rule changes.

## 3. Design decisions made (resolving original ambiguities)

These were unspecified in the original rules and were decided explicitly, since they materially change the data model:

1. **Jokers are wildcards from the start** — placeable directly into any meld when it's first laid down, not only via the explicit swap-out action. (§2.3, §2.4)
2. **Shared access, per-player layout — and full rearrangement, not just additions** (revised 2026-07-26; ownership-restricted 2026-07-27) — either player may add cards to any meld on the table regardless of who laid it, *and* may pull cards back out of a meld and re-lay them into different valid melds (standard Rummy-style manipulation), as long as every meld left on the table (untouched, shrunk, or newly formed) still satisfies the 3+-card set/run rules. **Pulling is restricted to cards the pulling player currently owns** — confirmed against the designer after a mismatch surfaced in play (§3 decision 11) — only *adding* is universal. Melds are grouped by owner on the table purely as a layout/display convention — "shared" describes access rights, not a single undifferentiated pile. Scoring tracks ownership per card, not per meld, and ownership follows the most recent act of placement; because pulling is ownership-restricted, this never lets rearrangement itself move credit between players (only joker swap-out does that). (§2.3, §2.8)
3. **Empty closed pile ends the round immediately** — no reshuffle of the open row into a new closed pile. If the closed pile runs out, the round ends right there and both hands are scored as-is (loser penalty rule still applies to whoever has cards; if both have cards, both take their own hand penalty and neither gets the +50 winner bonus). This makes hoarding cards late in a round genuinely risky.
4. **Adding to a meld (or rearranging the tableau) requires having come out, and cannot itself count as coming out** — closes the exploit an adversarial review found: without this, a player could score points off the opponent's melds forever without meeting the 40-point bar, or come out cheaply by dropping one card onto an opponent's existing triple. Laying additional *new* melds from hand pre-come-out is still allowed and is exactly how the cumulative 40-point total is reached (§2.4). (§2.3, §2.4)
5. **Retired 2026-07-26 — was wrong.** Previously: "a player who hasn't come out yet cannot draw from the open row at all." Confirmed against the actual game: the open row is available every turn regardless of come-out status (§2.3, §2.5). The deadlock this was meant to prevent (being forced to take cards with no legal way to meld them) never actually existed, because taking from the row is always a voluntary choice, never forced.
6. **No limit on repeat joker swap-and-replay** within a round, even though each swap moves up to 50 points of scoring between players — left as a deliberate skill/tempo battle rather than artificially capped. (§2.3)
7. **Turn 0 starter-card exchange rewritten as a standalone pre-rotation exchange** (§2.6) — fixes a self-contradiction in the original draft, where the second player was promised a single-card grace pickup that had already become physically impossible by the time their turn arrived under the normal cascade rule. **Revised again 2026-07-26 to be asymmetric**, not symmetric: the "second look" follow-up only exists on the path where P1 declines first and P2 takes the card — if P1 takes it immediately, Turn 0 ends right there.
8. **A Part 2 meld action can never leave a player at 0 cards in hand** (added 2026-07-26, replaces the old "mid-turn instant win" rule) — this is illegal outright, not merely undesirable. Structurally guarantees a round can only ever end via the Part 3 discard (§2.7), removing the need for a separate mid-turn ending case entirely.
9. **Open-row takes repeat within Part 1, but lock out the closed pile once used, and only the latest take is a binding obligation** (added 2026-07-26) — confirmed against the game's designer. The first row-take in a Part 1 forecloses the closed-pile option for the rest of that turn; further row-takes remain available and may repeat any number of times; each new row-take supersedes the previous one's "must meld" obligation rather than stacking obligations. The player explicitly ends Part 1 once done drawing. (§2.3)
10. **Come-out progress toward the 40-point bar carries forward across turns, not just within one turn** (added 2026-07-26, confirmed against the game's designer after the mock's live-score display made a below-threshold attempt visible in play) — a meld that falls short (e.g. a 30-point triple) isn't wasted; the next new meld that player lays, whenever that happens, only needs to make up the remaining difference. Tracked per player (§2.4).
11. **Pulling from the tableau is restricted to cards the pulling player currently owns** (added 2026-07-27, confirmed against the game's designer after a live-play report of pulling back a card the *opponent* had laid) — either player can still *add* to any meld regardless of who laid it, but *pulling* a card back out only works on cards credited to the puller themselves. This also means rearrangement never moves scoring credit between players on its own (§3 decision 2) — only a joker swap does that. (§2.3)
12. **Adding a card to a run may reposition an existing joker in that run, not just extend an end** (added 2026-07-27) — if the new card doesn't directly extend the low or high end, but repositioning a joker already in the run (reassigning what rank it stands for) would make the whole thing valid, that's allowed as one action rather than being rejected. Ownership of pre-existing cards, jokers included, is unaffected — only the newly added card is newly credited (§2.8). (§2.3)
13. **Full tableau rearrange session: draft-then-commit, distinct from the single-card pull** (added 2026-07-27) — a second, more powerful rearrangement mode alongside the ownership-restricted pull (§3 decision 11). Within a session, a player may freely regroup ANY card on the table — either player's — plus their own hand, into new groupings, including temporarily-illegal ones; nothing is validated until the player commits. On commit, every resulting group is checked; if any are invalid, the player sees specifically which and why, and can keep adjusting or cancel back to exactly the pre-session state. Two invariants keep this deliberately more permissive mid-session mode from reopening the exploit the pull restriction closed: (a) a pre-existing card **always keeps its original owner** no matter which group it ends up in at commit — only cards newly brought in from hand are credited to the committing player; (b) a pre-existing card that isn't the committing player's own can't simply be left in hand at commit — it must end up in some valid group. Requires having come out, and can't be started with a pending "must meld" obligation outstanding. (§2.3)
14. **Round 1's starter is decided by chance; every round after that, the starter alternates** (added 2026-07-27, confirmed against the game's designer after a live-play report that one player was always starting both new games and new rounds) — previously an unconfirmed assumption that defaulted to a fixed player always starting. Alternation is by round number, independent of who won the previous round. (§2.2)
15. **Turn 1 goes to the opponent of whoever made the last accepted swap in Turn 0, not automatically back to the starting player** (added 2026-07-27, confirmed against the game's designer) — taking the starter card and swapping "uses up" that go the same way a normal turn does. If nobody ever accepted a swap in Turn 0, the starting player begins Turn 1 as before; otherwise the last acceptor's opponent begins Turn 1. See §2.6 for the full case breakdown. (§2.6)
16. **A player may never take an action that would leave their hand at or below the number of cards still owed to an outstanding "must meld" obligation** (added 2026-07-27, found by adversarial review, not live play) — formalizes an invariant the game always implicitly needed but never stated. Without it, a row-take obligation (§2.5) or a joker swap-out obligation (§2.3) can end up as the only card(s) left in hand, at which point melding it is illegal (§3 decision 8, entire-hand melds are forbidden) but discarding is also illegal (obligations must clear first) — a genuine dead end with no legal move. This never changes what any individual action is allowed to *produce*; it only blocks the specific actions that would create that trap in the first place (e.g. swapping a joker with only that swap's replacement card left in hand, or melding away every other card while an unrelated obligated card sits untouched). (§2.3, §2.5)

## 4. Persona review notes (design-quality sanity check)

- **Experienced Rummy/Gin player**: the open-row cascade pickup is the one mechanic that isn't just "Rummy with different names" — it adds a bluffing layer (bury a card to bait a costly pickup) that neither Gin Rummy's single discard pile nor standard Rummy's draw pile has. Worth protecting as the game's identity in marketing, not undersold as "yet another rummy."
- **Novice player**: the ruleset is dense (four point tiers, a 40-point/four-of-a-kind come-out gate, the opening-turn exception sequence, cascade pickup obligation). None of this is learnable from a rulebook alone in an app — needs an interactive tutorial and an AI-assisted "legal move" highlight for early hands, not just a text rules screen.
- **Hardcore/competitive player**: wants difficulty tiers on the AI, a hand-history/replay view, and eventually ranked online play with stats. Noted there's no Gin-style scaling bonus for winning with the opponent still holding a lot of deadwood (flat +50 regardless) — this reads as a deliberate simplicity choice, not an oversight; worth stating as such rather than silently "fixing" it later.
- **Joker volatility (flagged by adversarial review, not originally caught by any persona)**: because jokers are wild, the tableau is shared, and a swapped-out joker must be replayed the same turn, the same physical joker can repeatedly change hands across a round, each swap worth up to 50 points. With only 2 jokers in 54 cards, this is likely the single biggest score-swing mechanic in the game — left uncapped by design (§3, decision 6), but worth deliberately play-testing for, since it could dominate final scores more than the melds themselves.
- **Tableau rearrangement does *not* extend the joker-volatility dynamic (corrected 2026-07-27)** — an earlier draft of this note claimed it did. Confirmed against the designer: pulling is restricted to cards the pulling player already owns (§2.3/§3 decision 2), so rearrangement can never move scoring credit from one player to the other by itself; it only lets a player reorganize their *own* placed cards. Joker swap-out remains the sole mechanic where credit shifts between players, and is therefore still the single biggest score-swing risk in the game (see the note above) — not compounded by rearrangement the way this note previously (incorrectly) suggested.

## 5. Software architecture

### 5.1 Core principle

Build the **rules engine as a pure, deterministic, framework-agnostic module** (TypeScript) that has no concept of "human" vs. "AI" vs. "network peer" — only "player 1" and "player 2," each driven by an adapter. This is the one decision that's expensive to reverse later, so it's made up front even though multiplayer is out of scope for v1.

Engine responsibilities (fully unit-testable in isolation, independent of any UI):
- Deck construction, shuffle, deal.
- Turn state machine (Part 1 → Part 2 → Part 3, enforcing legality at each step).
- Meld validation (sets, runs, joker-wildcard fills, come-out threshold check).
- Open-row cascade pickup logic (§2.5), including the Turn 0 starter-card exchange (§2.6).
- Per-card ownership tracking for scoring.
- Round-end and game-end detection.

This engine is the natural place to encode every rule ambiguity resolved in §3 — each should have a corresponding unit test using the exact scenario that made it ambiguous.

### 5.2 Solo phase (v1 target)

- **Stack**: Expo / React Native, matching Appiness's proven toolchain and release pipeline (GitHub-hosted APK for sideload testing → Expo EAS build → Google Play once stable).
- **AI opponent**: a heuristic bot to start (minimize deadwood value, prioritize meeting the come-out threshold early, avoid discarding into a position that hands the human a large, useful cascade). Runs entirely on-device, calling the same engine API a human player's UI would call — no special-cased "AI turn" path. A stronger AI (minimax/MCTS over meld and discard choices) is a nice-to-have for a later difficulty tier, not a v1 requirement.
- **Persistence**: local only — expo-sqlite or AsyncStorage for match history, stats, and running score across rounds within a game.
- **No backend required for v1.**

### 5.3 Multiplayer (later phase, architecture reserved for now)

Because the engine is side-effect-free and doesn't assume client authority, the **rules logic** can later run server-side without a rewrite:
- Clients send intents (draw / meld action / discard) over WebSocket; the server runs the same engine, validates each intent, and broadcasts the resulting state.
- This is the standard anti-cheat shape for hidden-hand card games — a client-authoritative model would let a modified client see the opponent's hand or fabricate a legal-looking move.
- Natural host: the existing LXC Docker host (192.168.2.115), a small Node service, simple 2-player room/matchmaking.

**Caveat (from adversarial review, not to overstate this):** "no rewrite" only covers the rules logic. It does **not** solve the actually-hard part of multiplayer for a hidden-information game — per-viewer state redaction (the server must serve each client a filtered view with the opponent's hand hidden), session/reconnect handling, and matchmaking are all still unbuilt and unaddressed here. A pure engine is a solid foundation, not a finished multiplayer design — no need to design the rest further until solo v1 is validated and multiplayer is actually being scoped.

Rationale: "Cascade" still names the actual distinguishing mechanic (the overlapping open row); "Break" now points at the tableau-rearrangement mechanic (§2.3, §3 decision 2) — pulling a meld apart and re-laying it — rather than at the 7-card deal, which "Sevens" named. No naming diligence (store title search, trademark, domain/handle) has been re-run yet for "Cascade Break" — treat it as unvetted, same "working title, not locked" caution as before.

**Why this is only a working title, not locked:** the diligence so far (done for "Cascade Sevens," not yet redone for "Cascade Break") was a couple of point-in-time web searches against Google Play title strings only. ## 7. Roadmap (proposed phases)

0. **Spike the cascade UI on a real phone screen — done (2026-07-23).** Built as a static HTML/JS hotseat web mock (human vs. a simple heuristic AI), covering the full ruleset rather than just the cascade interaction — `github.com/JJJeroen/CascadeSevens` (`docs/`), live at `jjjeroen.github.io/CascadeSevens/`. Also served its actual purpose: playtesting it surfaced the 2026-07-26 corrections above (open-row gating, Turn 0 asymmetry, mid-turn ending, tableau rearrangement) that no amount of design review alone had caught. Still true and still worth remembering for the eventual native UI: the open row can grow to 15–25+ overlapping cards by late-round, and tapping a specific buried card out of that on a small touchscreen remains a real interaction-design risk worth a dedicated look before committing to the Expo UI.
1. **Rules engine** — implement and unit-test the full ruleset above, including every §3 decision. Testing strategy: unit tests per rule (one per §3 decision, using the exact scenario that made it ambiguous), plus property-based/fuzz tests for invariants that should always hold (card count always conserves to 54; the AI never selects a move the legality-checker itself would reject), plus full-round simulation tests (engine plays itself out via the heuristic AI on both sides, thousands of times, checking nothing crashes or hangs). No UI yet.
2. **Local playable prototype** — bare-bones Expo UI, human vs. heuristic AI. **"Fun" gate, defined concretely rather than left as a vibe check:** a minimum number of full games actually played across multiple sessions (not just by whoever wrote the rules — that's a structurally biased n=1 sample), with specific friction points logged per session, before calling this phase done.
3. **Polish & tutorial** — proper UI/UX, interactive tutorial, legal-move highlighting, accessibility pass.
4. **GitHub sideload release** — APK for personal/friend testing, same as Appiness's pre-Play-Store phase.
5. **Google Play release** — via Expo EAS once stable. Needs an explicit privacy policy (required for the listing, especially once local stats/persistence exist) and store listing assets/copy as real tasks, not assumed-solved by the Appiness precedent — confirm what Appiness actually needed here rather than assuming it transfers.
6. **Multiplayer** — server-authoritative engine hosted on the LXC Docker host, WebSocket transport, matchmaking, and the hidden-hand state redaction the engine alone doesn't solve (§5.3). Scoped in detail only once v1 is live.

**Monetization**: deliberately left as an open decision, revisit later — not assumed free/no-ads by default.

## 9. Open questions

All four rules ambiguities originally listed here were resolved on 2026-07-23 — see the "(§9, resolved)" tags at §2.3 (Ace in runs) and §2.9 (tie-break, quick-game mode). **The 2026-07-23 mid-turn-instant-win resolution was itself wrong and was reversed 2026-07-26** — see §2.7 and §3 decision 8; a round now only ever ends via the Part 3 discard.

**2026-07-26 corrections, confirmed against the game's designer via a web-mock playtest** — all now folded into §2.3/§2.5/§2.6/§2.7/§2.8/§3: open-row pickup is available every turn regardless of come-out status, and may be taken repeatedly within Part 1 (locking out the closed pile once used, only the latest take binding as the obligation); the Turn 0 exchange's step-3 follow-up is asymmetric (only reachable via the P1-declines/P2-accepts path); a Part 2 meld action can never leave a player at 0 cards (illegal outright, replacing the old mid-turn-win rule); the tableau's "shared" rule was right about access but missed the actual mechanic — a full pull-and-relay rearrangement, not just additions, with ownership following the most recent placement; the app displays a live running score, not just a round-end lump sum.

**2026-07-27 corrections and additions**, all from live play rather than design review: come-out progress toward 40 carries across turns instead of resetting each turn (§2.4/§3 decision 10); pulling from the tableau is restricted to cards the puller already owns, not any card regardless of who laid it (§2.3/§3 decision 11) — this also retracted the earlier claim that rearrangement could shift scoring credit between players the way a joker swap can (it can't, under the ownership restriction); adding a card to a run may reposition an existing joker in that run to make room, not just extend an end (§2.3/§3 decision 12); a full draft-then-commit rearrange session was added alongside the single-card pull, letting any card on the table (either player's) plus hand be freely regrouped mid-session with validation deferred to commit (§2.3/§3 decision 13); round 1's starter is decided by chance and alternates every round after (§2.2/§3 decision 14), replacing a previous unconfirmed assumption that a fixed player always started; and Turn 1 now goes to the opponent of whoever made the last accepted Turn 0 swap rather than automatically back to the starting player (§2.6/§3 decision 15).

One item remains open, deliberately deferred rather than blocking:
- **AI difficulty tiers**: how many, and what specifically differs (heuristic weights vs. a genuinely stronger search-based opponent) — deferred to §5.2/roadmap phase 2+, not blocking v1 engine work.

Phase-0 roadmap item (§7) is done — see there for what shipped and what it caught.
